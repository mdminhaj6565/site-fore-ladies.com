Women's Golf Shoes: Where Comfort, Traction and Style Meet

A round of golf is a long walk with roughly seventy full-body rotations built into it. No other piece of equipment works as hard as your shoes, and none has as direct an effect on both comfort and ball striking. Get them right and you stop thinking about your feet entirely. Get them wrong and you are managing a blister from the sixth hole onwards. More about Shoes

Spiked or spikeless: which one is for you?

This is the first decision, and there is no universally correct answer. Spiked shoes use replaceable soft cleats that bite into turf. They give you the most traction on wet grass, on slopes and in the rough, and they are the safer choice if you play a hilly course or a links in the wind. The trade-off is that they are less comfortable on hard surfaces — cart paths, clubhouse floors, the car park — and the cleats need replacing periodically.

Spikeless shoes use a moulded outsole with lugs or dimples instead of removable cleats. They are lighter, they feel more like a trainer, and you can wear them from the car to the first tee to lunch without changing. On dry, flat courses they provide plenty of grip. On a wet, steep day they will eventually let go. Many golfers end up owning one of each and choosing by the forecast.

If you are buying your first pair and you mostly play in fair weather, spikeless is the easier, more versatile choice. If you play year-round in a climate with real rain, start with spiked.

Waterproofing is not optional in most climates

Wet feet ruin rounds faster than almost anything else, and morning dew soaks shoes just as thoroughly as rain does. Waterproof uppers — whether full-grain leather or a treated synthetic — keep that out. Pay attention to whether a shoe is described as genuinely waterproof or merely water-resistant, and to the length of any warranty the brand offers, because that warranty is usually the clearest signal of how confident the maker is.

Waterproofing does come at a small cost in breathability. If you play mainly in dry heat, a more breathable, lightly treated shoe will be more comfortable than a fully sealed one. This is another case where the honest answer depends on where you play rather than on which shoe is objectively better.

Fit: the part most people get wrong

Feet swell over the course of a round, which is why golf shoes are best fitted in the afternoon rather than first thing in the morning, and why they should have a little more room than your everyday shoes. Aim for roughly a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe. Your heel should not lift when you walk. The shoe should hold your midfoot firmly — that is what stops your foot sliding during the downswing — without pinching across the widest part.

Width matters as much as length and is far more often ignored. If you have tried several golf shoes and they all felt tight across the ball of the foot, the issue is almost certainly width rather than size, and going up a full size to compensate will simply give you a shoe that is too long. Look for brands that offer genuine width options, and check the size charts, since golf shoe sizing varies noticeably between European and American brands.

Fit the shoe with the socks you will actually play in. A cushioned golf sock can take up a surprising amount of room, and a shoe fitted with a thin liner will feel completely different on the course.

How shoes affect your swing

Your swing starts from the ground. The sequence that produces clubhead speed begins with pressure shifting through your feet, and anything that lets your foot slide inside the shoe leaks energy out of that sequence. A shoe that locks your heel and midfoot in place gives you something to push against. This is why golf shoes are built with more lateral support than running shoes — running is a straight-line activity, and golf very much is not.

Stability does not have to mean stiffness. Current women's golf shoes, including the Duca Del Cosma range, use lightweight materials and cushioned midsoles that absorb the impact of several miles of walking while still holding the foot. If you have previously found golf shoes clumpy compared to trainers, it is worth trying a recent pair; the category has changed considerably.

Style that works beyond the course

There is no longer any reason for golf shoes to look like golf shoes. Duca Del Cosma's Festiva, Queenscup, Caldes, Kubana, Boreal, Bellezza and Alexa styles span everything from clean white leather to navy, silver, red, light blue and printed finishes. Many pass comfortably as smart trainers, which matters if your round is followed by lunch or you are travelling to a course and would rather not pack two pairs.

Colour choice is practical as well as aesthetic. White and light shoes show grass stains and mud quickly but look crisp with almost any outfit. Navy, black and darker patterns hide wear and suit a wider range of conditions. If this is your only pair, a mid-tone or patterned shoe will look good for longer.

Caring for golf shoes so they last

Brush grass and soil off the outsole after every round, while it is still damp and comes away easily. Wipe the upper with a cloth. If the shoes are genuinely wet, let them dry slowly at room temperature with the insoles removed — never on a radiator or in direct sun, which cracks leather and can unglue soles. Shoe trees or loosely crumpled paper help them hold their shape while drying.

If you play in spiked shoes, check the cleats every few rounds. Worn cleats are a slip hazard and they wear unevenly, which subtly changes how you stand. Replacing them is inexpensive and takes minutes. Rotating between two pairs, if you have them, roughly doubles the life of both by letting each dry out fully.

Choosing your next pair

Start with where you play and in what weather, then narrow by spiked or spikeless, then by width, then by style. If you are between two sizes, it is worth trying both and keeping the one that fits — trying to make a nearly-right shoe work over a full season rarely ends well.
