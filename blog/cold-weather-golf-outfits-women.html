What to Wear Golfing in Cold Weather: A Layering Guide for Women

By Fore Ladies Golf team · October 25, 2026

To golf comfortably in cold weather, dress in three thin layers instead of one thick one: a moisture-wicking base layer, a warm mid-layer such as a quarter-zip or vest, and a wind-resistant outer jacket. Add golf pants or lined leggings under a skort, a warm hat, and winter golf gloves or mitts for between shots. Thin layers keep you warm without stopping your shoulders from turning.

Autumn and winter golf can be some of the most enjoyable of the year — quieter courses, crisp air, firm fairways. The trick is staying warm on the walk while still being able to make a full swing.

Why layers work better than one heavy coat

Layers trap warm air between them and let you adjust as the day warms up or the wind picks up. A single bulky coat restricts your rotation and leaves you with nothing to change once you are too hot after a hill climb.

The idea behind layered clothing is simple: each layer has one job. The base layer moves sweat away from your skin, the middle layer holds warmth, and the outer layer blocks wind and light rain.

Layer 1: the base layer

Your base layer should be close-fitting and moisture-wicking, so sweat from walking doesn't sit on your skin and chill you later. Avoid cotton here; it holds moisture.

  • Synthetic base layers (polyester or nylon blends with stretch) dry quickly and are easy to wash.
  • Merino wool base layers are soft, warm for their weight and resist odor over a long day.
  • A long-sleeve mock neck works as a base layer and as a top on its own when the sun comes out. See long sleeve golf tops.

Layer 2: the warm middle

The mid-layer is where most of your warmth comes from, and the best options leave your arms free. A quarter-zip pullover, a lightweight fleece or a knit golf sweater all work well.

A vest deserves a special mention. It keeps your core warm, which is what keeps your hands and feet warm, while leaving your shoulders completely free. Many golfers wear a vest over a long-sleeve base layer for most of the autumn. Browse golf pullovers, golf sweaters and golf vests.

Layer 3: wind and weather protection

Wind is what makes a cool day feel cold on an open golf course, so the outer layer should block it. A wind jacket or softshell golf jacket is ideal; it should be light, quiet when you swing, and roomy enough to fit over your mid-layer.

Check that the sleeves don't ride up at the top of your backswing and that the jacket isn't so long it bunches when you address the ball. If rain is likely, switch to a waterproof jacket. Not sure which type you need? Our guide to softshell, windbreaker and insulated golf jackets compares them side by side. See golf jackets and the full golf outerwear section.

Legs, feet and hands

Legs: golf pants are the simplest cold-weather choice, and some are lined or made of heavier, brushed fabric. If you love skorts, wear thermal leggings or tights underneath where the course allows it. See golf pants.

Feet: waterproof golf shoes keep morning frost and dew out. Add warm, moisture-wicking socks — merino blends are a good choice — and avoid socks so thick that your shoes become tight, which cuts circulation.

Hands: cold hands lose feel for the club. Many golfers keep hands in mitts or pockets between shots and wear winter golf gloves, sold as pairs, while swinging. Hand warmers in a pocket help on very cold days.

Head: a beanie or a lined cap with ear coverage keeps you noticeably warmer. A visor alone isn't enough once the temperature drops.

Cold-weather outfits by temperature

Everyone feels the cold differently, and wind and sun change everything, so treat this as a starting point.

Small things that make a big difference

  • Start a little cold. Walking warms you up within a few holes; if you are cozy on the first tee you will probably be too warm by the third.
  • Use pockets wisely. Keep your golf balls in an inside pocket. Many golfers find cold balls feel harder and don't travel as far, so a warm ball helps.
  • Carry a dry glove. A spare glove in a zip pocket is a lifesaver after a damp front nine.
  • Drink water anyway. You still lose fluid in cold, dry air, even if you don't feel thirsty.

Frequently asked questions

How cold is too cold to play golf?

There is no single cut-off; it depends on wind, sun and how prepared you are. Many golfers happily play in the 40s °F with good layers. Below freezing, courses often delay tee times because frozen greens are easily damaged, so check with the course before you drive over on a frosty morning.

Can I wear leggings for winter golf?

At many public courses, yes, especially in cold weather. Stricter clubs may not allow leggings on their own, but thermal leggings under a skort or golf dress are usually accepted. Lined golf pants are the most widely accepted option if you are unsure about the course rules.

What should I wear on my hands for cold-weather golf?

Wear a pair of winter golf gloves designed for grip in the cold, and keep your hands in mitts or warm pockets between shots. Hand warmers in a pocket help on very cold days. Swap to a dry glove if your first pair gets damp, because wet gloves make cold hands much worse.

Is a vest or a jacket better for golf?

A vest is better when it is cool but not cold, because it keeps your core warm and leaves your arms completely free. A jacket is better once wind or temperature makes your arms cold. Many golfers carry both and switch as the day changes.

With three thin layers, warm hands and a hat, a cold-weather round becomes something to look forward to rather than endure. For damp days, see our rain gear guide.
