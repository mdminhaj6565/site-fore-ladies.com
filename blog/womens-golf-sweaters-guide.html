Golf Sweaters for Women: Merino, Cotton or Synthetic?

By Fore Ladies Golf team · November 5, 2026

The best golf sweater for most women is a fine-gauge merino or wool-blend knit for cool, active rounds, a cotton or cotton-cashmere sweater for dry, mild days when you want a classic look, and a synthetic or stretch knit when you need something easy-care that dries fast. Whatever the fiber, a golf sweater should be thin enough to swing in, shaped to sit at your waistband, and roomy enough for a polo or base layer underneath.

Here is how the main fabrics compare, and how to choose one you will actually wear on the course.

Why wear a sweater for golf instead of a jacket?

A sweater gives you steady, quiet warmth with almost no restriction, which is why it works so well on cool, calm days. A jacket is better when wind or rain is the real problem.

Knits stretch naturally in every direction, so a well-fitted sweater moves with your shoulder turn instead of pulling across your back. They don't rustle at the top of your backswing. On a breezy day you can add a wind shell over the top; our guide to softshell, windbreaker and insulated golf jackets explains which one pairs best.

The trade-off: most sweaters let wind through and soak up rain, so treat them as your warm middle layer.

Merino wool golf sweaters: warm, breathable and forgiving

Merino is the most versatile fiber for golf sweaters because it keeps you warm without trapping sweat. It is a good pick if you walk the course or play rounds that start cold and warm up by the back nine.

Merino comes from a breed of sheep known for very fine wool, which is why it feels soft rather than scratchy. The wool industry body Woolmark describes merino fibers as absorbing large amounts of moisture vapor and moving it away to evaporate, and says the fiber reacts to changes in body temperature. Woolmark also says merino holds onto odor molecules until the garment is washed. That is the wool industry describing its own fiber, but it explains why outdoor brands use merino for base layers.

What that means on the course: a merino sweater tends to feel comfortable across a wider temperature range, so you are less likely to be peeling it off on the fourth hole and pulling it back on at the turn. Look for a fine or medium gauge (the tightness of the knit); chunky knits look cozy but add bulk under your arms.

Many golf sweaters blend wool with other fibers. The Daily Sports cable knit lined pullover in our catalog, for example, is listed as 30% wool and 70% acrylic with a half-length zip and high collar, a mix that keeps some of wool's warmth while making the knit lighter and easier to care for.

Cotton and cashmere-blend sweaters: classic, soft, best when dry

Cotton and cotton-cashmere sweaters are the classic preppy golf look, and they are lovely on dry, mild days. They are less forgiving if you sweat a lot or the weather turns damp.

Cotton is soft and breathable, but it soaks up moisture and dries slowly, so a cotton knit can feel heavy and cool once it gets wet from sweat or drizzle. Adding cashmere, the fine undercoat fiber of cashmere goats, makes the sweater softer and warmer for its weight.

Our catalog shows how common these blends are in golf. The Daily Sports cable knit sweater is described as a slim-fit V-neck in 95% cotton and 5% cashmere, while the Daily Sports Bolzano V-neck uses 85% cotton and 15% cashmere in a relaxed fit.

Synthetic and stretch knits: easy-care and quick to dry

Synthetic golf sweaters, made from fibers such as acrylic, polyester or viscose blends, are usually the lightest, stretchiest and easiest to wash. They suit golfers who want a machine-washable layer that won't need special handling.

Acrylic was developed as a wool substitute: it is light, warm for its weight and holds color well, though it doesn't manage moisture as well as wool. Stretch knits with polyester or elastane add swing freedom. The Scratch Seventy knit V-neck in our catalog is described as a stretch knit in a viscose and polyester blend made for easy movement.

The downside: synthetics can hold odor more than wool, and cheaper knits may pill.

Which sweater style works best for golf?

V-necks and quarter-zips are the easiest golf sweater styles because they show your collar and pass almost any dress code. Crew necks and roll necks work too, especially with a mock-neck top underneath.

  • V-neck: the classic choice over a collared polo. The collar sits neatly on top, which keeps strict clubs happy.
  • Quarter-zip or half-zip: lets you vent heat without taking the layer off. Lined versions add warmth for early tee times. See our golf pullovers for more zip styles.
  • Crew neck: pair it with a collared top if your course requires collars.
  • Roll neck or turtleneck: warm on cold, still days and usually accepted at golf courses, but check your club's rules first.
  • Sweater vest: keeps your core warm and your arms completely free. Browse golf vests if long sleeves feel restrictive.

Dress codes vary by course, so check yours. Our women's golf dress code guide covers what most clubs expect.

How should a golf sweater fit?

A golf sweater should be close enough to layer under a shell but loose enough that you can make a full turn without the back pulling. The hem should cover your waistband at address and not ride up at the finish.

  • Size for what goes underneath. A thin polo needs less room than a base layer plus a polo.
  • Check the brand's measurements against our women's golf size charts; slim and relaxed fits from the same brand can differ by a full size.
  • Make a few slow practice swings. If the shoulders bind or the sleeves slide over your glove, try another cut.

For full outfits by temperature, from a single sweater to winter base layers, see our cold-weather golf layering guide.

How to care for golf sweaters

Always follow the care label, wash knits cold and gently, and dry them flat rather than in a dryer. Hanging a wet sweater stretches the shoulders out of shape.

For cashmere and fine wool, Apartment Therapy's cashmere washing guide recommends cold water with a wool or gentle detergent, a short soak, squeezing water out instead of wringing, rolling the sweater in a towel and laying it flat to dry. Frequently asked questions

Is merino or cotton better for a golf sweater?

Merino is usually better for active golf because it handles sweat and changing temperatures well and stays warmer if it gets damp. Cotton feels soft and looks classic but holds moisture and dries slowly. If you walk the course or play in variable weather, choose merino or a wool blend; for dry, mild cart rounds, cotton works well.

Can you wear a sweater over a golf polo?

Yes, and it is one of the most traditional golf looks. A V-neck sweater over a collared polo lets the collar show, which satisfies most dress codes. Make sure the sweater is thin enough to swing in and that the polo's collar lies flat on top rather than getting tucked inside the neckline.

Are turtlenecks allowed on the golf course?

Turtlenecks and mock necks are accepted at most courses, especially in cold weather, and both are common in women's golf collections. Some traditional private clubs still prefer a collared shirt, so check your club's dress code before you play. Wearing a collared polo under a crew-neck sweater is a safe alternative.

Will a golf sweater keep me warm in the wind?

Not on its own. Most knits let wind pass through, so a sweater feels much colder on a breezy day. Add a thin windbreaker or softshell over it, or choose a lined sweater, which has an inner layer that blocks some wind. On calm, cool days a good sweater is often all you need.
