Women's Golf Bags: Choosing the One That Suits How You Play

A golf bag is the most personal piece of equipment you own that is not a club. It decides how much you carry, how organised you are, how your back feels at the end of eighteen holes, and — quietly — how put-together your whole set-up looks on the first tee. This collection covers the full range: cart bags and stand bags from Sun Mountain, coordinated carry-alls, shoe bags and totes from Glove It, belt bags from Daily Sports, and travel and lifestyle bags from Orca.

More about Bags

Start with one question: do you ride or walk?

Almost every other decision follows from this. If you ride a cart for most rounds, a cart bag is the right choice. Cart bags are built to sit securely on a cart with the pockets facing outward and accessible while the bag is strapped down, and because you are not carrying the weight, they can afford to be larger, better organised and more heavily padded. Sun Mountain's cart bags — the Boom 14-way, the Mid-Stripe 14-way and the Diva among them — give you a divider for every club, which prevents the shaft-on-shaft rattle that wears grips and paint.

If you walk, weight becomes the dominant concern, and a stand bag is what you want. Stand bags carry legs that deploy when you set the bag down, dual straps that spread load across both shoulders, and a deliberately lighter build. A Sunday bag such as the Sun Mountain SLx goes further still, stripping the bag back to a handful of clubs and the essentials for a quick nine or a range session.

If you do both, be honest about the split. A cart bag carried for one round will convince you never to walk again; a minimal stand bag on a cart leaves you fishing for pockets all day.

Dividers, pockets and what actually matters

Club dividers are counted as 4-way, 6-way, 14-way or full-length. More dividers means less club-on-club contact and easier retrieval, but also more weight and a slightly narrower opening for each club. A 14-way top is a genuine luxury on a cart bag. On a walking bag, 4- or 6-way with full-length dividers is usually the better compromise.

Pockets deserve more attention than they usually get. Look for a full-length apparel pocket big enough for a rain jacket and a pullover, an insulated pocket for water, a lined valuables pocket for a phone and keys, and at least one pocket you can reach while the bag is strapped to a cart. A rangefinder pocket with a magnetic closure is worth having if you use one, because it saves you zipping and unzipping on every approach.

Strap quality is the detail people regret ignoring. If you walk, a properly padded dual strap with an adjustable sternum connection transforms how a loaded bag feels after twelve holes. Single straps are lighter and fine for Sunday bags; for a full set, take the dual strap.

Belt bags and carrying less

There is a growing category of very small bags designed to hold only what you need between shots, and they solve a real problem. The Daily Sports Hobart belt bag holds a phone, a spare ball, tees and a marker on your hip, so you are not walking back to the cart every time you need something. If you play scramble formats, or you like to walk ahead to your ball while a partner drives, a belt bag is genuinely useful rather than merely fashionable.

The same thinking applies to crossbody and 2-zip carry-all bags from Glove It. They are sized for a lesson, a range hour, or the drive to the course, and they are far more pleasant than emptying a full golf bag into a handbag.

Shoe bags, towels and the supporting cast

A dedicated shoe bag keeps damp, grass-covered spikes away from clean clothes, and away from the interior of your car. Glove It's Augusta and Sea Glass shoe bags come in the same prints as their larger bags, so the whole set coordinates if that appeals to you. Pair one with a sport towel, a belt wristlet and a matching cap or visor and you have a complete, deliberate-looking kit without having thought about it very hard.

Orca's travel and lifestyle bags cover the other end of the spectrum — the duffel that goes on the golf trip, the piece that has to survive an airport rather than a fairway. If you travel to play, a proper travel cover for your clubs is not optional; airline handling is not kind to graphite shafts.

Weight, balance and your back

A fully loaded walking bag weighs a surprising amount once you add clubs, balls, water, a jacket and an umbrella. Two things reduce the strain more than any marketing feature. First, balanced weight distribution: the bag should hang close to your back rather than pulling away from it, which is what a well-designed dual strap and a good hip pad achieve. Second, ruthless editing — most golfers carry several items they never use. Emptying your bag completely at the start of each season and only putting back what you actually reached for last year is the single most effective weight reduction available.

If carrying is uncomfortable regardless, a push cart is the middle path between carrying and riding. Sun Mountain's Speed Cart V1R models fold compactly and take the entire load off your shoulders while still letting you walk the course, and our push cart collection includes seats and accessories to go with them.

Matching your bag to the rest of your kit

Golf bags last years, so it is worth choosing one you will still like. Coordinated ranges — Glove It's Augusta and Sea Glass collections, for example — let you match a bag, a cap, a visor, a towel and a shoe bag without guessing at colours. If you prefer a bag that disappears rather than announces itself, Sun Mountain's black, camo and neutral colourways go with everything and hide wear well.

A practical tip: if you buy a light-coloured bag, be realistic about maintenance. Bags pick up grass, mud and sunscreen. Wiping the base and the bottom of the pockets after a wet round keeps a pale bag looking new far longer than it otherwise would.

Care and storage

Empty the bag at the end of the season. Old balls, damp towels and half-eaten snacks are how a bag starts to smell. Wipe the interior, leave the pockets open to air, and store the bag upright somewhere dry rather than flat in a garage where moisture collects. Check zips occasionally; a little lubricant on a stiff zip prevents the forced tug that eventually breaks it.
